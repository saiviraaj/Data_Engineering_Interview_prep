# The Manager Mindset — Complete Guide
## From Individual Contributor to Engineering Manager
## For Costco Round 3 + All Future Leadership Interviews

---

# THE FUNDAMENTAL TRUTH ABOUT MANAGEMENT

Before anything else, understand this clearly:

```
ENGINEERING MANAGER ≠ SENIOR ENGINEER + PEOPLE ADMIN

Most engineers think becoming a manager means:
  Doing all your current technical work PLUS
  Doing performance reviews and 1:1s on top

That is wrong. And that framing leads to bad managers.

WHAT MANAGEMENT ACTUALLY IS:

Individual Contributor: Your output = what you personally produce
Manager: Your output = what your TEAM produces

That sounds similar but it changes everything:

IC asks: "How do I solve this problem?"
Manager asks: "Who on my team is best positioned to solve this problem,
               and what do they need from me to succeed?"

IC succeeds by being technically excellent.
Manager succeeds by making others technically excellent.

IC removes blockers for themselves.
Manager removes blockers for 6-10 people simultaneously.

IC writes code.
Manager writes the conditions for good code to be written.

THE HARDEST PART OF TRANSITIONING:
  Letting go of technical execution.
  Watching someone solve a problem differently than you would — 
  and not taking over.
  Finding satisfaction in their success, not in writing the code yourself.
  
  This transition takes most engineers 6-12 months to fully make.
  The ones who don't make it become "managers who code" —
  they're neither good ICs nor good managers.
```

# PART 1: THE ENGINEERING MANAGER'S JOB

## 1.1 What an EM Actually Does

```
CORE RESPONSIBILITIES (in order of long-term impact):

1. GROWING PEOPLE (highest leverage, longest time horizon)
   - Identify each engineer's growth trajectory
   - Create opportunities that stretch them appropriately
   - Give honest, specific feedback (positive and corrective)
   - Advocate for promotions when deserved
   - Have career conversations, not just status updates
   
   Why highest leverage: a well-developed engineer produces value for years.
   A badly managed engineer stagnates or leaves.

2. SETTING DIRECTION (high leverage, medium horizon)
   - Translate business goals into technical priorities
   - Make the roadmap visible and understandable to the team
   - Align team work to what matters most to the organization
   - Protect the team from thrash and constant reprioritization
   
   Why critical: a team without clear direction works hard and produces noise.

3. REMOVING BLOCKERS (high leverage, short horizon)
   - Identify what is slowing engineers down
   - Clear organizational friction (approvals, dependencies, unclear requirements)
   - Make decisions when the team is stuck
   - Be available when engineers need clarity
   
   Why critical: a blocked engineer is an expensive resource producing nothing.

4. BUILDING CULTURE (high leverage, very long horizon)
   - Model the behaviors you want to see
   - Celebrate the values you want to reinforce
   - Address behaviors that contradict team values immediately
   - Build psychological safety (people speak up, don't hide problems)

5. HIRING (outsized leverage when done right)
   - One great hire can change the team's trajectory for years
   - One bad hire costs months of your time and team morale
   - This is where managers have the most long-term leverage
   
WHAT A MANAGER SHOULD NOT DO:
  ✗ Be the best engineer on the team (hire people better than you)
  ✗ Make all technical decisions (that's the tech lead's job, or delegates)
  ✗ Be the bottleneck for all communication
  ✗ Shield the team from all difficult feedback (they need it to grow)
  ✗ Micromanage (it signals distrust and kills ownership)
```

## 1.2 The Manager's Calendar — How Time Should Be Spent

```
HEALTHY MANAGER TIME ALLOCATION:

PEOPLE (40%):
  1:1s with direct reports (weekly, 30 min each)
  Career conversations (monthly)
  Performance feedback (ongoing, not just review cycle)
  Hiring interviews (2-3 hours/week when hiring)
  
DIRECTION AND ALIGNMENT (30%):
  Roadmap planning and communication
  Cross-team dependencies (syncing with PMs, other EMs)
  Stakeholder communication (leadership updates, business alignment)
  Sprint planning and review facilitation
  
TECHNICAL (20%):
  Architecture and design reviews
  Code review (high-leverage areas, not all PRs)
  Technical strategy (where are we headed?)
  Staying current on technology trends
  
OPERATIONS (10%):
  Incident management and postmortems
  Reporting, metrics, status updates
  Process improvements

COMMON TIME ALLOCATION MISTAKE:
  New managers spend 70% on operations (meetings, status reports) 
  and 5% on people development.
  
  Then they wonder why their team isn't growing and their best 
  engineers are leaving.
  
  If you're spending more than 20% on operations:
  → Something is broken in process (too many unnecessary meetings)
  → Or you're doing work that should be delegated
```

# PART 2: 1:1s — THE MOST IMPORTANT TOOL

## 2.1 What a Great 1:1 Looks Like

```
WHAT A 1:1 IS:
  A weekly 30-minute meeting between a manager and one direct report.
  It is the employee's meeting, not the manager's.
  
WHAT A 1:1 IS NOT:
  ✗ A status update on current tickets (that's what standups are for)
  ✗ A performance review (that happens formally quarterly/annually)
  ✗ A meeting for the manager to assign work
  ✗ A meeting the manager can cancel when "busy" (that signals people don't matter)

THE AGENDA (employee-driven):
  Employee comes with their topics.
  Manager's questions create space for what employee might not bring up.
  
WHAT TO COVER IN A 1:1:
  
  HOW ARE THEY? (5 minutes)
  "How are you doing this week? Anything on your mind?"
  This is not small talk. This is emotional temperature check.
  A burned-out engineer won't tell you in standup.
  They might tell you here.
  
  WHAT'S BLOCKING THEM? (10 minutes)
  "What's the hardest thing you're dealing with right now?"
  "What are you waiting on that I can help unblock?"
  Your job: remove friction. This is where you find out about it.
  
  CAREER AND GROWTH (10 minutes, not every 1:1)
  "Are you working on something that's stretching you?"
  "Is there a skill you want to develop that you're not getting to practice?"
  "How are you feeling about your growth trajectory here?"
  
  FEEDBACK BOTH WAYS (5 minutes)
  You give feedback to them.
  Critically: you ask for feedback on yourself.
  "Is there anything I'm doing that's making your work harder?"
  "Is there anything I should be doing differently?"
  
  A manager who never asks for feedback will never get honest 
  feedback from their team. And they will be blindsided.

WHAT TO WRITE DOWN:
  Notes from each 1:1 in a shared doc (you and them both have access).
  Commitments made (you said you'd do X, write it down and do it).
  Patterns over time (are they consistently frustrated about the same thing?).

THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE ABOUT 1:1s:
  Never cancel them.
  Rescheduling once in a while: fine.
  Repeatedly canceling: sends the message "I don't have time for you."
  This is how you lose good engineers.
```

## 2.2 Giving Difficult Feedback — The SBI Framework

```
THE SINGLE BIGGEST FAILURE MODE OF NEW MANAGERS:
  Avoiding difficult conversations.
  
  Why it happens: discomfort with conflict.
  What it costs: the person never improves, the problem compounds, 
                 other engineers see you tolerating it and lower their own bar.

THE SBI FRAMEWORK (Situation → Behavior → Impact):

SITUATION: Specific context where the behavior occurred.
  Not: "You always miss deadlines."
  Yes: "In the sprint planning meeting on Tuesday..."

BEHAVIOR: What the person actually did (observable, not interpretation).
  Not: "...you were being difficult."
  Yes: "...you committed to delivering the pipeline by Friday 
       but didn't mention the blocker until the following Monday."

IMPACT: What effect that behavior had (on the team, project, or business).
  Not: "...and that was unprofessional."
  Yes: "...which meant the BI team couldn't run their reports as planned 
       and had to delay their stakeholder presentation."

FULL EXAMPLE:
  Bad feedback: "You need to communicate better."
  SBI feedback: "In the sprint planning meeting on Tuesday, you committed 
                 to delivering the pipeline by Friday but didn't mention 
                 the dependency on the API team until Monday morning. 
                 This meant the BI team couldn't run their reports as 
                 planned and had to delay their stakeholder presentation. 
                 In the future, if you see a blocker forming, I need to 
                 know within 24 hours so we can clear it before it causes 
                 cascading delays."

ADDITIONAL RULES FOR DIFFICULT CONVERSATIONS:
  
  Do it promptly:
  Feedback about something that happened 3 weeks ago is useless.
  Give feedback within 24-48 hours of the behavior.
  
  Do it privately:
  Never give corrective feedback in front of the team.
  Praise publicly. Correct privately.
  
  Listen to their side:
  After you share the observation: "What was happening from your side?"
  Often there's context you didn't know.
  The conversation should be a dialogue, not a lecture.
  
  End with clarity:
  What changes do you expect?
  By when?
  What support will you provide?
  
  Document:
  After the conversation, send a brief follow-up:
  "Thanks for talking through the deployment process today. 
  To summarize: [what was discussed, what was agreed]."
  Not as a paper trail for firing — as a record of the agreement.
```

# PART 3: HIRING — THE HIGHEST-LEVERAGE MANAGEMENT ACTIVITY

## 3.1 The Hiring Philosophy

```
RULE 1: ONE GREAT HIRE > THREE ADEQUATE HIRES
  A great engineer multiplies team output.
  An adequate engineer consumes team energy (mentoring, review, coordination).
  Never hire to fill a headcount. Hire to raise the team's level.

RULE 2: HIRE FOR CEILING, NOT CURRENT PERFORMANCE
  Don't just evaluate: "Can they do the job today?"
  Evaluate: "Where is their ceiling? Are they still growing?"
  An engineer whose growth has plateaued will be a low performer in 2 years.

RULE 3: CULTURE AND VALUES MATTER AS MUCH AS SKILLS
  Skills can be learned. Values rarely change in adults.
  A brilliant engineer who doesn't share the team's values is a net negative.
  Technical interviews miss this. Behavioral interviews surface it.

RULE 4: MAKE THE BAR EXPLICIT BEFORE INTERVIEWING
  Write down: what does "bar met" look like for each interview dimension?
  If interviewers have different bars, you get inconsistent decisions.
  The hiring bar is a management responsibility, not an individual interviewer's call.

RULE 5: DECIDE QUICKLY
  The best candidates are interviewing at multiple places simultaneously.
  A week of deliberation often means losing the candidate to a faster decision.
  "Strong hire" decisions should close within 48 hours of final interview.
```

## 3.2 Designing a Good Interview Process

```
DATA ENGINEERING INTERVIEW STAGES:

STAGE 1: RECRUITER SCREEN (30 min)
  Goal: Verify basic fit (compensation, location, timeline)
  Not a technical screen. Fast filter.

STAGE 2: TECHNICAL PHONE SCREEN (60 min)
  Goal: Is there genuine technical competence?
  Format: 2-3 SQL or Python problems, real-world focused
  Bar: "Would I be comfortable with this person touching production code?"

STAGE 3: TAKE-HOME OR TECHNICAL DEEP DIVE (2-4 hours)
  Goal: How do they approach a real problem?
  Format: Build a small pipeline, or debug a broken one, or design a schema
  What to look for:
    - Do they ask clarifying questions?
    - Do they handle edge cases (nulls, duplicates, schema evolution)?
    - Is their code readable without a comment on every line?
    - Do they test their work?

STAGE 4: SYSTEM DESIGN (60 min)
  Goal: Can they design systems at scale?
  Format: "Design a real-time ad metrics system" (or similar)
  What to look for:
    - Structure (do they clarify requirements first?)
    - Trade-off reasoning (do they consider alternatives?)
    - Practicality (would this actually work in production?)

STAGE 5: BEHAVIORAL / CULTURAL (45 min)
  Goal: Values, collaboration, growth mindset
  Format: STAR questions
  What to look for:
    - Do they take ownership or blame others?
    - Do they acknowledge weaknesses honestly?
    - Do they talk about growth or just achievement?
    - How do they handle conflict?

STAGE 6: HIRING MANAGER 1:1 (30 min)
  Goal: Direct assessment of fit, answer the candidate's questions
  Not another technical screen — they've passed that.
  Questions to ask:
    "What would make this role the best job you've had?"
    "What would make you leave a role within the first year?"
    "What kind of engineering culture makes you do your best work?"

DEBRIEF PROCESS:
  All interviewers submit written feedback independently BEFORE the debrief.
  Debrief: share feedback, discuss disagreements, make a decision.
  No "strong hire" from one interviewer overrides a "no hire" from another 
  without discussion. Everyone's data matters.
```

# PART 4: PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT

## 4.1 The Performance Review Cycle

```
HOW PERFORMANCE REVIEWS FAIL:
  - Annual reviews: engineer is surprised by feedback they got once a year.
  - All feedback at review time: too late to change anything.
  - Generic feedback: "good job, keep it up." Useless.
  - Recency bias: last month counts more than 11 months before.

HOW TO DO PERFORMANCE REVIEWS WELL:

ONGOING:
  Give feedback in real-time (within 24-48 hours of observed behavior).
  Track feedback in 1:1 notes. This becomes your review evidence.
  Quarterly "how are we doing" conversation: no surprises at annual review.

BEFORE THE REVIEW:
  Ask engineer to self-assess: "What are you most proud of? 
  What areas do you want to grow in?"
  Gather peer feedback (2-3 people who worked closely with them)
  Review your 1:1 notes from the year.

THE REVIEW CONVERSATION:
  Not a monologue where you read out ratings.
  A dialogue: "How do you feel about your performance this year?"
  Often the engineer's self-assessment is harsher than yours.
  Or reveals blind spots they don't see.
  
  Structure:
  1. What you're proud of (specific examples)
  2. Where growth happened (specific examples)
  3. Where you want to see more growth (specific, with support plan)
  4. What we're working toward together (career trajectory, next level)

CALIBRATION:
  Before finalizing ratings, managers calibrate with each other.
  Goal: ensure an "exceeds expectations" in one team 
  means the same thing as "exceeds expectations" in another team.
  
  Calibration also surfaces: is there a pattern of bias? 
  (e.g., consistently rating certain demographics lower)
```

## 4.2 Managing a Low Performer

```
THE WORST THING A MANAGER CAN DO:
  Ignore poor performance hoping it gets better on its own.
  It doesn't. It gets worse. And it damages team morale.
  
  Team members NOTICE when poor performance is tolerated.
  They conclude: "Why should I work hard? No one gets held accountable here."
  
  The manager who avoids difficult conversations is not being kind.
  They are being cowardly, and they are hurting the whole team.

THE PERFORMANCE IMPROVEMENT FRAMEWORK:

STEP 1: DIAGNOSE (before anything else)
  What is the root cause of the underperformance?
  
  Skills gap → mentoring, training, pairing. Not a firing offense.
  Motivation/engagement gap → career conversation. Find what they want.
  Role misfit → maybe they're in the wrong role. Lateral move might solve it.
  Personal circumstances → temporary situation. Be compassionate, give space.
  
  The conversation: "I've noticed X over the last few weeks. Help me understand 
  what's happening from your side."
  
  Don't assume. Diagnose first.

STEP 2: BE EXPLICIT (if the problem is performance, say so clearly)
  "I need to be direct with you: your current output is not meeting the 
  expectations for your level. This is not sustainable. I want to work 
  through it together, but I need you to understand it's serious."
  
  Many managers dance around this. 
  The engineer thinks everything is fine.
  Then they're on a PIP and completely blindsided.
  This is a manager failure, not an engineer failure.

STEP 3: CREATE A PLAN
  Specific expectations: what does "meeting the bar" look like? 
  (not "communicate better" but "respond to blocking issues within 24 hours")
  Timeline: 4-6 weeks to see improvement
  Support: what will you do to help? (pairing, training, check-ins)
  Milestones: what should be different at week 2, week 4?

STEP 4: FOLLOW THROUGH
  Check in weekly.
  Give feedback immediately when you see improvement (positive reinforcement).
  Give feedback immediately when you see the old pattern (immediate correction).
  
  Document everything. Not to build a termination case, but because 
  an undocumented agreement is not an agreement.

STEP 5: DECISION POINT
  If performance has improved: acknowledge it explicitly. 
  "I want to say clearly — the improvement I've seen in the last 3 weeks 
  is exactly what I was asking for. Thank you for engaging with this seriously."
  
  If performance hasn't improved: this is the hardest part of management.
  A PIP (Performance Improvement Plan) is the formal process.
  HR involvement is required at this stage.
  The outcome may be termination — and that is a legitimate management tool.
  A team that keeps a persistent low performer loses its best performers.
```

# PART 5: TEAM HEALTH AND CULTURE

## 5.1 Building Psychological Safety

```
WHAT IS PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY?
  "The belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up 
  with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes."
  — Amy Edmondson (Harvard Business School)

WHY IT MATTERS FOR ENGINEERING TEAMS:
  
  LOW psychological safety:
  Engineers don't report problems early.
  Engineers don't ask for help (fear of looking dumb).
  Engineers don't disagree with the tech lead.
  Small problems become big incidents.
  The best engineers leave first (they have options).
  
  HIGH psychological safety:
  Engineers report blockers and bugs immediately (smaller blast radius).
  Engineers ask questions → better decisions, less rework.
  Engineers disagree respectfully → better technical decisions.
  Mistakes become learning, not blame.
  The team retains good engineers because they feel valued.

HOW TO BUILD IT:

1. Model vulnerability yourself
   Say "I don't know" when you don't know.
   Share mistakes you've made and what you learned.
   Ask for feedback and act on it visibly.
   If engineers see you model fallibility: they'll feel safe being fallible too.

2. Respond to bad news with curiosity, not blame
   When something breaks: first question is "What happened?" not "Who did this?"
   Post-mortems are blameless. System failures, not people failures.
   
3. Celebrate questions and disagreements
   "That's a great question I don't have the answer to."
   "I'm glad you pushed back on that — you were right."
   Make it visible that speaking up leads to good outcomes.

4. Take action on what you hear
   If people raise concerns and nothing changes, they stop raising concerns.
   You don't have to solve everything. But you have to acknowledge, respond,
   and explain what you can and can't change.

5. Eliminate status and hierarchy in technical discussions
   "The most senior engineer in the room is not automatically right."
   "The intern's question might be the question that saves us from a bad decision."
   Grade the argument, not the arguer.
```

## 5.2 Handling Team Conflict

```
TYPES OF CONFLICT IN ENGINEERING TEAMS:

TYPE 1: HEALTHY DEBATE (good — don't eliminate it)
  Two engineers disagree about the right technical approach.
  This is productive friction. Let it happen.
  Your role: ensure it's about ideas, not identity ("my approach" vs "the right approach")
  Resolution: data, prototype, ADR documenting the decision

TYPE 2: PROCESS FRICTION (annoying but fixable)
  "The deployment process takes too long and nobody fixes it."
  "Design reviews are a blocker, not a help."
  Your role: take it seriously, investigate, fix what's actually broken.
  Don't dismiss: "That's just how it is."

TYPE 3: INTERPERSONAL CONFLICT (hardest)
  Two engineers don't work well together.
  This requires direct intervention.
  
  Step 1: Talk to each person separately.
  Get their perspective without the other present.
  "Tell me what's been happening with you and [person]."
  Listen without taking sides.
  
  Step 2: Name the impact, not the people.
  "When two engineers on the same team can't collaborate effectively, 
  the whole team slows down. That's what I'm seeing."
  
  Step 3: Bring them together (if appropriate)
  "I'd like the three of us to talk about how we want to work together."
  Mediator role: focus on behaviors and working agreements, not personalities.
  
  Step 4: If it doesn't resolve:
  Sometimes the right answer is restructuring who works with whom.
  Not always possible — but if two people genuinely can't collaborate,
  one of them may need a different team.
  
  The worst outcome: tolerating conflict that poisons team culture.
  Everyone else is watching how you handle it.
```

# PART 6: TRANSITIONING FROM IC TO MANAGER — THE ROADMAP

## 6.1 What to Expect in the First 90 Days

```
THE FIRST WEEK:
  Don't change anything.
  Listen, observe, build context.
  Meet with every direct report 1:1 within week 1.
  Questions to ask each one:
  "What's going well that we should protect?"
  "What's not working that we should fix?"
  "What do you need from me that you're not currently getting?"
  
  Meet with your manager.
  "What does success look like at 30/60/90 days?"
  "What are the most important things I should be focused on?"
  "What do you need from me to trust me with this team?"

THE FIRST MONTH:
  Keep running existing processes (standups, retrospectives, planning).
  Establish your 1:1 cadence. Don't skip a single one.
  Identify: who are the strongest engineers? Who needs the most support?
  Find one quick win — something broken that you can fix fast.
  This builds credibility: "our new manager actually listens and acts."

THE FIRST 90 DAYS:
  Understand the full technical landscape.
  Meet key stakeholders (PMs, other EMs, business leaders).
  Start forming your view on technical direction.
  Make one meaningful, visible improvement.
  Build relationships before making structural changes.
  
  RULE: No major structural changes in first 90 days.
  You don't have enough context yet.
  Changes made without context break trust fast.

COMMON FIRST-TIME MANAGER MISTAKES:
  
  ✗ DOING THE TECHNICAL WORK YOURSELF
  You're not the best IC anymore. Let engineers solve problems.
  Your value is in enabling them, not in doing it yourself.
  
  ✗ TRYING TO PLEASE EVERYONE
  As a manager, some decisions will disappoint someone.
  Prioritization means some things don't get done.
  That's the job. Make the call, explain the reasoning, move on.
  
  ✗ NOT HAVING DIFFICULT CONVERSATIONS
  Every avoided conversation becomes a bigger problem.
  Give feedback immediately. Address conflict directly.
  
  ✗ LOSING TECHNICAL CREDIBILITY
  Going fully into management and losing all technical context.
  You need enough technical depth to earn respect and make good decisions.
  Stay close enough to the work to know when something doesn't make sense.
  
  ✗ TREATING 1:1s AS STATUS MEETINGS
  The most common mistake. Engineers hate this.
  If you run 1:1s as status updates, engineers will dread them.
  Use them for growth, feedback, and blockers. Not Jira review.
```

## 6.2 Interview Questions About Moving Into Management

---

**Q: "You've been an IC for 11 years. Why management now?"**

```
ANSWER:

"The honest answer is that I've been thinking about this for about 2 years. 
Not because I've become bored with technical work — I haven't — but because 
I've noticed where my actual leverage comes from.

When I write a good pipeline, I create one good pipeline.
When I mentor an engineer on how to design pipelines well, 
I create 5 years of well-designed pipelines.
When I establish architecture standards for a team of 8, 
I shape the quality of every decision that team makes for years.

That's a fundamentally different kind of leverage.
And I've been operating closer to that model on CDM Next — 
driving standards, mentoring, aligning teams, making architectural decisions 
that shaped what 60 teams built. I wasn't the manager, but I was doing 
much of the work that creates team-level impact.

I'm not moving to management to stop being technical. 
I'm moving because I want my technical judgment to operate 
at the level of a team or organization, not just my own output.

What I'm clear-eyed about: there are things I haven't done yet.
Performance reviews. Difficult conversations about someone's future here.
Hiring decisions. I don't have experience making those calls directly.
But I understand what they require, I've observed good managers doing them,
and I'm ready to learn by doing — with appropriate support."
```

---

**Q: "How would you handle an engineer who is more technically skilled than you?"**

```
ANSWER:

"This is actually the goal, not a problem to handle.

As a manager, my job is not to be the best engineer on the team.
My job is to build a team where the engineering quality is higher 
than it would be if I were an individual contributor.

That often means hiring and developing engineers who are deeper 
in specific areas than I am. The Dataflow expert who knows the 
Beam internals better than I do. The BigQuery performance specialist 
who can diagnose query plans I wouldn't catch. That's not a threat —
that's the team working.

My value in that relationship is:
1. Connecting their expertise to the right problems
2. Removing the organizational friction they don't have time for
3. Translating their technical concerns into business language 
   for stakeholders
4. Advocating for their growth and recognition
5. Protecting their time to do deep technical work

What I avoid: pretending to know things I don't know.
If someone is more technically expert in an area: say so.
'You have more depth here than I do — what's your recommendation,
and what do you need from me to implement it?'

An engineer's trust in their manager is built partly on honesty 
about the manager's own limitations. A manager who fakes expertise 
loses trust permanently when they're found out."
```

---

**Q: "How do you build trust with a team quickly as a new manager?"**

```
ANSWER:

"Three things I would do in the first 30 days.

First: listen before acting.
I would do 1:1s with every engineer in week 1 and ask:
'What's working well here that I should protect?'
'What's not working that I should fix?'
'What do you need from me that you haven't been getting?'
I take notes. I don't make commitments I can't keep.
But I demonstrate I'm here to understand, not to impose.

Second: find one visible problem and fix it fast.
Every team has something that's been annoying them for months 
that nobody fixed. A broken CI step. An unclear definition of done. 
A recurring meeting that has no purpose.
Fixing one concrete thing signals: 'this manager actually listens and acts.'
It builds more trust than a 30-minute 'I'm excited to be here' intro.

Third: be transparent about what I don't know.
When I don't have an answer: 'I don't know, I'll find out by Thursday.'
When I make a mistake: acknowledge it openly.
When I change my mind based on feedback: say why.

Trust is built through consistent behavior over time, not through 
any single action. The foundation is: 
do what you say, say what you mean, be honest when you're wrong.
Engineers are very good at detecting inauthenticity. 
You can't build trust by performing trustworthiness. 
You have to actually be trustworthy."
```

# PART 7: MANAGER INTERVIEW QUESTIONS AT ALL LEVELS

```
Q: "What's your management philosophy in one paragraph?"
A: "My job as a manager is to create the conditions for excellent engineering 
    to happen consistently — not to be the best engineer myself. That means: 
    growing people deliberately (not just assigning work), making technical 
    direction clear so engineers can make good decisions autonomously, 
    removing blockers before they compound, and building a culture where 
    people surface problems early because they know they won't be punished 
    for it. I measure my own success by my team's performance and 
    development, not by what I personally contribute technically."

Q: "How do you retain top engineers?"
A: "Three things retain engineers: doing meaningful work, growing professionally, 
    and being respected. I can influence all three. Meaningful work: connect 
    their daily tasks to the business impact — engineers who understand WHY 
    they're building something work differently than those who just execute 
    tickets. Growth: give them work that's slightly above their current level 
    — not so hard they fail, not so easy they stagnate. And respect: give 
    them autonomy, credit their ideas publicly, fight for their compensation 
    and promotions. The engineers who leave are usually the ones who feel 
    invisible or stagnant. My 1:1 notes tell me early if either is happening."

Q: "How do you handle an engineer who wants to be promoted but isn't ready?"
A: "Honestly and specifically. 'You're not ready yet' is not useful feedback. 
    'Here's exactly what I need to see to advocate for your promotion' is. 
    I use the promotion criteria explicitly: 'At senior level, we expect 
    independent delivery and cross-team influence. In the last quarter, 
    I've seen the first but not the second. Here's what that would look like, 
    and here's a specific opportunity to demonstrate it.' I set a timeline: 
    'Let's revisit in 2 quarters with this evidence.' If they do the work 
    and they're still not promoted: I fight for them with data. The credibility 
    of my feedback depends on whether I actually go to bat when they deliver."

Q: "What's the hardest part of being a manager?"
A: "The hardest part is the mismatch between what you can control and what 
    you're accountable for. I'm accountable for team outcomes. But I can't 
    write the code myself or make engineers make good decisions. All I can do 
    is create conditions. And some days those conditions produce excellent 
    engineering, and some days they don't, and it's not always clear why. 
    Learning to be okay with that — to keep showing up and doing the work 
    without needing to control every outcome — is genuinely hard for engineers 
    who are used to writing code where at least the machine does exactly 
    what you tell it to."

Q: "How do you handle a team that resists change?"
A: "I first ask why. Resistance to change is usually rational from the 
    team's perspective. They've seen initiatives before that promised 
    improvement and delivered complexity. I respect that. My approach: 
    don't announce the change as a decision — bring them into the problem. 
    'Here's what I'm seeing that makes me think we need to change X. 
    Does this match what you're seeing? What am I missing?' 
    When people help design the solution, they own it. 
    When it's handed down from above, they comply at best and resist at worst. 
    The one thing I don't do: move forward on a change without understanding 
    the resistance. There's usually real information in it."
```

# SUMMARY: THE MANAGER'S CHEAT SHEET

```
YOUR JOB:
  Your output = your team's output. Not your personal code.
  
THE 5 CORE RESPONSIBILITIES:
  1. Grow people (highest leverage)
  2. Set direction (align team to business goals)
  3. Remove blockers (protect engineering time)
  4. Build culture (psychological safety, ownership)
  5. Hire well (one great hire > three adequate hires)

1:1s:
  Weekly. 30 min. Employee's agenda.
  Never cancel. Cover: how are they?, blockers, growth, mutual feedback.
  
FEEDBACK:
  SBI framework: Situation → Behavior → Impact
  Give it within 24-48 hours. Privately. With a clear ask for change.
  
HIRING:
  Hire for ceiling and values, not just current skills.
  Make the bar explicit before interviewing.
  Decide fast. Best candidates don't wait.

PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT:
  Diagnose before prescribing.
  Be explicit about performance gaps — not hinting.
  Document agreements. Follow through.
  Never avoid the difficult conversation.
  
CULTURE:
  Model what you want to see.
  Respond to bad news with curiosity, not blame.
  Take action on what you hear.
  
THE TRANSITION FROM IC:
  First 90 days: listen before acting.
  Let engineers solve problems.
  Find and fix one visible pain point quickly.
  Be honest about what you don't know.
```
